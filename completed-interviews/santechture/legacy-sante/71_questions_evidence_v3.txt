CATEGORY 2 — BUSINESS AND RCM UNDERSTANDING

6. What do you understand about SANTECHTURE's business?

I see SANTECHTURE as a healthcare revenue-cycle technology company focused on helping providers improve financial outcomes through better workflow, rules, automation, integration and intelligence.

The business value is not simply submitting claims. It is preventing problems earlier: improving documentation and coding quality, validating requirements, reducing manual work, reducing denials and making cash flow more predictable.

From an architecture perspective, that means combining deterministic medical, coding, payer and regulatory rules with AI-assisted capabilities, while integrating with HIS, EHR, payer and government platforms.

The key business question for architecture is simple: does the platform make customer onboarding faster, regulatory change safer, integrations more reusable, operations more reliable and revenue outcomes better? If architecture does not improve those things, it is not creating enough value.

7. What is the business value of architecture in an RCM company?

Architecture should reduce the cost and risk of growth.

If every new hospital, payer, market or regulation requires a separate custom solution, growth becomes slower and more expensive. Strong architecture creates reusable core capabilities and isolates the parts that must vary.

I have seen the same principle in my own work. In national and enterprise integrations, the difficult part is not building one interface; it is creating boundaries, contracts, data ownership, error handling, observability and support models that can survive change.

So I would measure architecture through business results: onboarding time, integration reuse, release lead time, change failure rate, incident impact, support effort, performance, and the amount of customer-specific code we avoid creating.


CATEGORY 3 — PRE-SALES AND STRATEGIC DEALS

8. What is your experience with pre-sales?

My experience is mainly on the technical and solutioning side of pre-sales rather than carrying a sales quota or owning commercial closing.

I have worked with senior business stakeholders, government entities, partners and vendors to understand needs, run discovery and solution discussions, assess feasibility, define architecture and integration approaches, identify dependencies and risks, support estimation and evaluate build-versus-buy choices.

For example, in programmes such as SAP and Dynamics 365, I was involved from business and integration requirements through architecture, technical acceptance and production transition. That requires the same discipline that good technical pre-sales needs: understand the real problem, make assumptions visible, explain trade-offs and avoid promising something engineering cannot support.

I would rather surface a constraint early than win a deal based on a promise that damages trust later.

9. Walk me through your pre-sales approach for a new enterprise customer.

I start with discovery, not with a demo.

First, I want to understand the business outcome, current workflow, systems landscape, integration points, data ownership, transaction volumes, security and regulatory constraints, operational pain and success criteria.

Then I map the need against existing product capabilities. I separate what can be configured, what needs integration, what is a genuine product gap and what may be a customer-specific request.

After that, I define the solution architecture, assumptions, dependencies, NFRs, implementation approach, risks, rough sizing and what a successful PoC or first phase would prove.

My goal is not to match every requested feature. It is to show a solution that creates value for the customer and is realistic for SANTECHTURE to build, operate and support.

10. How do you translate a technical solution into business value for a CEO or CFO?

I start with the business outcome and use technology only to explain how we will achieve it.

For example, I would not tell a CFO that we need asynchronous messaging because it is modern architecture. I would explain that it helps isolate external-system failures, reduces blocked processing and protects transaction continuity.

I would not describe reusable integration adapters as a technical achievement. I would connect them to faster hospital onboarding, lower implementation effort and lower support cost.

This is how I already communicate in my current role: I present strategy, budget priorities, operational risks, modernization roadmaps and build-versus-buy trade-offs to senior leadership. The executive conversation should always be about value, risk, cost, time and measurable outcome.

11. Sales promised something Engineering says cannot be delivered on time. What do you do?

First, I remove emotion and establish the facts: what exactly was promised, what the customer truly needs, what engineering can deliver safely, and which date or requirement is actually fixed.

Then I bring Sales, Product, Architecture and Engineering together around options. We may phase the scope, reuse an existing capability, change the implementation approach or offer an alternative that achieves the same business outcome.

I am used to coordinating business, technical and third-party dependencies in programmes such as Dynamics 365, where integration, testing and production readiness all had to move together.

If the commitment is genuinely impossible or creates unacceptable risk, I will say that clearly. Protecting credibility with the customer is more important than protecting an unrealistic promise.

12. A strategic customer requests a customization that could damage the product architecture. Do you accept it?

I separate the customer’s business need from the technical implementation they requested.

First, I look for a configuration option, a reusable extension point or a product capability that can solve the need without changing the core in a customer-specific way.

If it is genuinely unique, I evaluate strategic value, revenue impact, implementation cost, support burden, security and long-term architecture debt. I also ask whether the capability may be useful for other customers or markets.

My own architecture work is based on explicit system boundaries and decision rights. I have learned that one shortcut inside the core can become a permanent cost every time the platform changes.

So I will work hard to solve the customer’s problem, but I will not casually trade long-term product health for a short-term customization.

13. How do you run a technical proof of concept?

A PoC should answer a specific uncertainty, not become a small uncontrolled project.

I start by defining the question we are trying to prove, the success criteria, representative data, scope, time box, security boundaries and the decision that will follow.

I am comfortable using PoCs as part of technology selection and build-versus-buy decisions. In my current architecture governance, PoCs are one input alongside capability fit, TCO, lifecycle cost and architecture risk.

At the end, I document what was proven, what was not, what risks remain and what must change for production in areas such as security, scalability, observability, performance, testing and supportability.

14. How do you estimate a solution when requirements are incomplete?

I do not hide uncertainty behind a precise number.

I separate known scope from unknown scope, write the assumptions, identify external dependencies and estimate with the engineering owners who will deliver the work.

If the unknowns are material, I use a range and recommend discovery, data profiling or a technical spike before making a hard commitment.

This is especially important in integration work. In SAP and Dynamics programmes, data mapping, master-data quality, third-party dependencies, testing and production transition can change effort significantly if they are not understood early.

The objective is not to look precise on day one. It is to make uncertainty visible and reduce it systematically.

15. When would you say no to a customer?

I would say no when saying yes creates unacceptable security, regulatory, operational or architectural risk.

I would also say no when a request creates disproportionate long-term damage to the product compared with its customer value.

But I do not like a conversation that ends with only “no.” I explain the constraint, identify the real business outcome and propose a safer option, a phased approach or a reusable alternative.

In my current role, I have to balance business demand with security controls, production readiness, TCO and continuity. That same discipline is important in a product company: protect the customer relationship without creating a future problem for the customer or for SANTECHTURE.

16. How do you move a PoC into production without carrying PoC technical debt?

I treat the PoC as evidence, not automatically as production code.

Before production, I revisit system boundaries, security, data governance, performance, scalability, error handling, monitoring, test coverage, deployment, support ownership, recovery and operational readiness.

Some components may be production-quality and reusable; others should be redesigned or rebuilt.

This is very similar to how I govern major production transitions today. For MedIQ, SAP and Dynamics, “done” included validation, cutover, monitoring, supportability, reconciliation and stabilization—not only working code.

The production decision should be based on engineering standards and operational readiness, not on the fact that the demo succeeded.

17. Who owns the solution: Sales, Product, Architecture or Engineering?

Ownership is shared, but decision rights must be explicit.

Typically, Sales owns the commercial opportunity and customer relationship. Product owns product direction and prioritization. Solutions Architecture owns feasibility, end-to-end technical design and cross-product integrity. Engineering owns implementation quality and technical execution. PMO owns delivery coordination.

I would not treat that as a rigid diagram on day one. I would first understand SANTECHTURE’s current operating model and make sure the boundaries are clear enough that decisions do not fall between teams.

One of my operating principles today is explicit ownership: accountable owners, decision rights and escalation paths should be visible. That becomes even more important in a matrix product organization.


CATEGORY 4 — LEADERSHIP STYLE, DELEGATION AND MANAGEMENT

18. What is your leadership style?

My leadership style is mainly situational, with a strong bias toward clarity, ownership and outcomes.

With strong senior people, I give context, expected outcomes and decision boundaries, then give them room to execute. With someone who is still developing, I use more coaching, closer checkpoints and faster feedback.

Today I lead a 54-person multidisciplinary technology organization with seven direct reports across applications, analysis, QA and reporting, DevOps, mobile, data and integration, support, and infrastructure and security oversight. That scale only works if capable people have real ownership.

During a serious incident or time-critical decision, I can become more direct because clarity and speed matter. The style can change; accountability does not.

19. Which management approaches do you actually use?

I do not use one management method for every situation.

My default is situational leadership combined with management by outcomes: clear objectives, clear ownership, measurable results and regular feedback.

I use coaching when capability can be developed, delegation when the person is ready, collaborative decision-making when team knowledge matters, and more direct leadership during incidents or urgent situations.

I also use operating mechanisms, not only leadership style: architecture and design reviews, release gates, service KPIs, incident and RCA discipline, clear escalation, and post-production learning.

For me, management is not a label. It is choosing the right level of direction, support and governance for the person, the risk and the situation.

20. How do you manage senior technical people without micromanaging them?

I manage senior technical people through context and guardrails, not task-by-task control.

I make the business outcome, architecture principles, important NFRs, decision boundaries and risks clear. Then I expect them to own the detailed design and execution.

I stay involved in major architecture reviews, critical incidents, security or reliability decisions and changes that are difficult to reverse. I do not need to approve every technical detail.

That is how I operate today across a large multidisciplinary organization. My role is to keep technical context strong enough to challenge decisions and diagnose problems while implementation ownership remains with engineering teams.

If every senior engineer needs my approval for normal decisions, I have designed a bottleneck.

21. How do you delegate?

I delegate based on capability, risk, context and development opportunity.

I define the expected outcome, decision authority, constraints, escalation conditions and checkpoints. Then I give the person enough space to actually own the work.

I keep closer ownership of decisions that have major cross-company, regulatory, security or irreversible impact, while delegating decisions that teams can safely make within agreed guardrails.

With seven direct reports and a 54-person organization, delegation is not optional. It is how you create scale.

I also use delegation deliberately to build future leaders, not only to reduce my workload.

22. How do you stay technically relevant without becoming a bottleneck?

I stay technically relevant through architecture reviews, major incidents, technical metrics, system boundaries, integration contracts and production-readiness discussions.

I still understand the technology deeply enough to challenge designs and ask useful questions, but I do not try to become the implementation owner.

My recent work includes modernization from established .NET Framework and Telerik solutions toward React, Node.js services, C#/.NET APIs, PostgreSQL, Redis, Docker and Apache Superset, as well as enterprise integrations and private-cloud operations.

The goal is not to prove that I can still code everything. The goal is to keep enough technical depth to make better leadership decisions without taking ownership away from the engineering teams.

23. How do you motivate a team?

I do not assume everyone is motivated by the same thing.

Some people want autonomy, some want technical growth, some want recognition, some want career progression and some want stability and clarity.

At team level, I focus on clear priorities, real ownership, removing blockers, fair decisions and visible recognition for meaningful contribution. I also try to connect work to impact so people understand why it matters.

In my current operating model, stronger teams are built by setting direction and guardrails while capable people retain delivery ownership.

I believe motivation is not created by speeches. It is created by good leadership conditions, trust and meaningful work.


CATEGORY 5 — PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT AND TEAM DEVELOPMENT

24. How do you handle an underperforming employee?

I start by diagnosing the real cause before deciding on the action.

The issue may be capability, unclear expectations, workload, motivation, behavior, or a mismatch between the person and the role.

I use specific examples and evidence, agree on measurable expectations, define the support or coaching needed and set a clear review period. I follow up regularly rather than waiting until the end.

If the person improves, that is the best outcome. If performance does not improve after fair support and clear expectations, I take the appropriate formal action.

In a large team, avoiding an underperformance problem is not kindness. It transfers the cost to the stronger people and weakens accountability.

25. What if the underperformer has critical domain knowledge and has been with the company for years?

Critical knowledge changes the transition plan, not the performance standard.

I would first reduce the key-person risk by documenting the knowledge, pairing the person with others, transferring ownership and creating operational runbooks or architecture documentation where needed.

In parallel, I would address the performance issue with the same clarity and fairness as any other case.

I have a strong bias toward knowledge transfer because systems become fragile when one person is the only person who understands them.

The right answer is not to tolerate poor performance because the organization is dependent on one individual. The right answer is to remove the dependency while handling performance professionally.

26. Your strongest engineer is toxic and damaging team morale. What do you do?

I would address it directly and early.

A strong engineer creates value through both technical output and the effect they have on the team. If their behavior damages collaboration, trust or retention, then the total impact may be negative.

I would give specific examples, explain the impact, set a clear expectation for behavior and give the person a fair opportunity to change.

I would also make sure technical disagreement is not being incorrectly labeled as “toxicity.” Strong challenge is healthy; disrespect, intimidation or repeated destructive behavior is not.

If the behavior continues after clear feedback and support, I would act. I would not allow one high performer to lower the standard for the entire team.

27. How do you develop future leaders?

I develop future leaders by giving people increasing levels of real ownership.

That means giving them decisions, not only tasks: leading an architecture review, owning a release, handling a stakeholder discussion, mentoring another engineer or presenting a recommendation.

I stay available as a coach, but I do not take the responsibility back the moment a problem appears.

With a 54-person organization and seven direct reports, I need leaders below me who can run their areas with confidence.

I also look for people who can explain trade-offs, build trust across teams and take responsibility when something goes wrong. Technical strength matters, but leadership potential is broader than technical depth.

28. How do you hire a solution architect?

I hire a solution architect for thinking quality, communication and judgment—not only for the number of technologies on the CV.

I look for strong fundamentals in distributed systems, integration, data, security, reliability and NFRs. I also test whether the person can move from a business problem to a clear architecture and explain trade-offs.

I would use a realistic case rather than only interview trivia: incomplete requirements, multiple systems, an external dependency and competing business constraints.

I also look for humility. A strong architect should know when to go deep, when to ask specialists and when the simplest design is better.

The best solution architects connect customer value, engineering reality and long-term product health.


CATEGORY 6 — CONFLICT MANAGEMENT AND CROSS-FUNCTIONAL LEADERSHIP

29. Two senior architects strongly disagree. How do you resolve it?

First, I make sure the two architects are solving the same problem and using the same assumptions.

Then I define the decision criteria: business need, scalability, reliability, security, cost, delivery time, operational complexity, data impact and reversibility.

I ask both sides to present evidence and trade-offs. I move the discussion away from “my technology is better” toward “which option best fits this problem?”

If we still cannot agree, the accountable decision owner makes the decision, the reasoning is documented and the team commits.

I welcome strong technical disagreement. I do not allow it to become personal or stay open forever.

30. Product wants the feature now, Engineering says three months, and Sales promised six weeks. What do you do?

I would bring the three functions back to the same business problem and make the constraints explicit.

What exactly must be available in six weeks? What is the minimum outcome the customer needs? Why does engineering estimate three months? Which parts are technically risky or operationally unsafe?

Then we create options: phased scope, a smaller first release, reuse of an existing capability, a temporary supported approach or a changed customer commitment.

I have managed programmes where business process, integration, third parties, testing and production readiness all had to stay aligned. The answer is usually not to pick one department’s date; it is to redesign the plan around the real outcome and risk.

The final commitment must be one the company can own.

31. PMO says Architecture is slowing delivery. How do you respond?

I would take the feedback seriously because architecture can become a delivery bottleneck if governance is too heavy or too late.

I would look at where delay is happening. Are reviews happening only after teams have already designed the solution? Are standards unclear? Are too many decisions being escalated? Are we reviewing low-risk work with the same depth as high-risk work?

My preferred model is lightweight governance early in the lifecycle: principles, reference patterns, clear decision rights and fast escalation for exceptions.

In my current organization, architecture governance is connected to engineering and operations through reviews, validation, release readiness and post-go-live learning. The goal is to prevent rework, not create paperwork.

32. What if your team disagrees with your decision?

I want my team to disagree when they have evidence.

I ask them to explain the risk, assumptions and alternative. If their argument is stronger, I will change my decision.

If I still believe my decision is correct, I explain the reasoning and the decision criteria so people understand why, even if they would choose differently.

For important decisions, I document the decision and what would cause us to revisit it.

I do not need agreement on every decision. I need a culture where people can challenge safely, decisions are made clearly and the team commits after the decision.

33. How do you lead in a matrix organization when people do not report directly to you?

In a matrix organization, influence comes from clarity, credibility and shared outcomes more than reporting lines.

I make the objective, dependencies, decision rights and escalation path explicit. I spend time understanding what each function is trying to protect—revenue, product roadmap, delivery date, technical quality, customer trust or operational stability.

Then I frame decisions around the company outcome, not around my department’s authority.

I already work across business units, external partners, government stakeholders and technology functions that do not all report directly to me.

The key is to be consistent, transparent and useful enough that people want Architecture involved before a problem becomes an escalation.

34. How do you handle conflict between two team members?

I deal with team conflict early.

I speak to each person, understand the facts and separate technical or work disagreement from assumptions about intent.

Then I bring the discussion back to the shared objective, clarify roles and decision rights, and set the expectation for professional behavior.

If the same conflict keeps returning, I look for a structural cause: overlapping ownership, unclear priorities, incentives or poor communication.

I do not want artificial harmony. Healthy disagreement is useful. But repeated personal conflict consumes team energy and has to be managed.


CATEGORY 7 — CUSTOMER ESCALATIONS AND EXECUTIVE COMMUNICATION

35. A strategic customer is angry and says your product caused a major problem. What do you do?

First, I take ownership of the response before trying to prove who is technically correct.

I would stabilize the situation, establish severity and business impact, create a single communication channel and make sure the customer knows who owns the incident.

Then we work from evidence: logs, traces, transaction IDs, timing, recent changes and dependency status. We communicate what we know, what we do not know and when the next update will come.

My current role includes incident escalation, RCA, continuity and production-readiness governance, so I am used to treating operational trust as part of technology leadership.

After recovery, I want a proper RCA with corrective actions, ownership and prevention—not only a technical explanation.

36. The customer says SANTECHTURE is the problem, but your engineers say the customer's HIS is the problem. What do you do?

I would not allow the discussion to become “our system versus your system.”

We need an end-to-end transaction view: what entered SANTECHTURE, what we processed, what we sent, what the HIS returned, timestamps, payload identifiers, retries and errors.

The architecture should make that evidence available through trace IDs, structured logs and integration monitoring.

I have dealt with cross-system reconciliation in SAP and Dynamics integrations, and the same principle applies: establish the transaction truth first.

If the problem is ours, we own it. If it is on the customer side, we show the evidence professionally and help them resolve it. The objective is service restoration and trust, not winning the argument.

37. Would you admit to a customer that your architecture was wrong?

Yes.

If the architecture is wrong, I would rather admit it early and explain the recovery plan than protect my ego.

I would be precise about what is wrong, the customer impact, immediate containment, the target correction, the timeline and how we will reduce the chance of recurrence.

I would not use language that creates unnecessary fear or speculation before facts are known. But once the evidence is clear, transparency is part of leadership.

Customers can usually accept a technical mistake more easily than they accept hiding, delay or repeated surprises.


CATEGORY 8 — ARCHITECTURE AND PLATFORM SCALE

38. How would you scale SANTECHTURE across different hospitals and GCC markets?

I would design for one strong core and controlled variation.

The core product should contain the capabilities that are common across customers and markets. Regional, payer and hospital differences should be isolated through configuration, rules, adapters, terminology mappings and clearly defined extension points.

This is close to the way I approach national platforms and enterprise integration today: define boundaries, data ownership, interface contracts and NFRs so variation does not spread everywhere.

I would also create reusable onboarding patterns, certification tests and observability for each integration type.

My principle would be: localize at the edge, standardize at the core. Hospital number one hundred should be easier to onboard than hospital number ten.

39. How would you handle dozens of HIS, EHR, payer and regulatory integrations?

I would avoid uncontrolled point-to-point integration.

I would define clear integration contracts, canonical business concepts where useful, versioned APIs and events, reusable adapters, validation, security, idempotency, error handling and end-to-end traceability.

Different systems can still require different mappings, but the internal product should not become coupled to every customer’s data model.

This is an area where I have direct experience. In the SAP Strategic Medical Warehouses integration, I led UPA-side boundaries, data flows, mappings, master-data harmonization, security, error handling, logging, UAT, go-live and production reconciliation. I have also governed integrations across Dynamics 365, CRM, GS1/GLN and HMIS.

The architecture must make the next integration cheaper and safer than the previous one.

40. How would you modernize our legacy architecture?

I would not start by saying, “We need microservices.”

First, I would identify where the current architecture is hurting the business: slow releases, tight coupling, scaling limits, reliability issues, difficult testing, customer-specific change or high operational cost.

Then I would modernize incrementally around business capability boundaries, using the Strangler pattern where appropriate and protecting production continuity.

I have already led this type of transition: modernizing established .NET Framework and Telerik solutions toward React, Node.js services, C#/.NET APIs, PostgreSQL, Redis, Docker and Apache Superset while keeping operations running.

The goal is not more services. The goal is lower cost and risk of change, better ownership and safer delivery.

41. Microservices or modular monolith?

I support microservices where independent scale, ownership, deployment or fault isolation clearly justify them.

But I do not believe every mature product should be decomposed into many services just to satisfy an architecture label.

A well-designed modular monolith with strong boundaries can be better than poorly designed distributed microservices.

My decision would consider domain boundaries, team ownership, transaction consistency, performance, operational maturity, observability and the real need for independent deployment.

The architecture choice should reduce complexity for the business and engineering organization, not move complexity from code into the network without a good reason.

42. How would you manage millions of rules and frequent regulatory changes?

I would treat rules as governed product assets, not scattered code.

Each rule should have clear ownership, version, effective dates, market or payer scope, source or rationale, tests, audit history and rollback capability.

Changes should move through automated regression tests and controlled deployment. Customer-specific configuration must be separated from the shared rule core.

My own background includes translating regulatory and governance requirements into RBAC, segregation of duties, audit trails, traceability, retention and controlled approvals. The same governance mindset applies to a large rules platform.

When rules affect claims and revenue, we need to know exactly which rule version made which decision and why.

43. How would you support both cloud and on-premise deployments without fragmenting the product?

I would aim for one product architecture with multiple deployment profiles, not separate products.

The business logic, contracts and release model should remain as consistent as possible. Infrastructure differences should be isolated through deployment automation, configuration, secrets management, observability interfaces and supported platform abstractions.

I have current exposure to private-cloud operations, containerized services, PostgreSQL production and replica architecture, Redis, load-balancing controls, monitoring and backup and recovery oversight.

That helps because on-premise support is not only packaging software differently. It changes upgrade, monitoring, security, support and recovery responsibilities.

The objective is to respect data-residency and customer constraints without creating a permanently fragmented codebase.


CATEGORY 9 — AI, AUTOMATION AND GOVERNANCE

44. Where should AI be used in SANTECHTURE, and where should it not?

I would use AI where probability and pattern recognition create clear value, and deterministic logic where correctness and auditability must be guaranteed.

Good AI use cases include denial-risk prediction, anomaly detection, documentation assistance, coding suggestions, prioritization, knowledge retrieval and engineering productivity.

I would keep regulatory constraints, hard eligibility rules, security controls and final irreversible financial actions behind deterministic guardrails unless there is a very strong governance model.

The architecture should include confidence thresholds, explainability, human review where needed, audit trails and model monitoring.

AI should improve a controlled workflow. It should not become an unobservable decision-maker inside a financial healthcare process.

45. Would you let AI automatically modify or submit claims?

I would not give an AI model unrestricted authority to modify and submit claims.

I would classify actions by risk.

Low-risk suggestions or corrections with deterministic validation may be automated. Medium-risk changes may require confidence thresholds and human confirmation. High-impact or ambiguous changes should remain human-approved.

Every automated action should be traceable: what input the model saw, what it changed, confidence, rule checks, model version and final outcome.

The objective is to gain automation without losing accountability. In healthcare finance, speed is valuable, but an unexplainable wrong action can create regulatory, revenue and trust problems.

46. How would you introduce AI-assisted coding across the engineering organization?

I would introduce AI-assisted engineering as a governed productivity capability, not as an uncontrolled tool rollout.

First, I would define approved tools, data-handling rules, what code or customer information may be shared, review expectations and how generated code is tested.

Then I would start with useful low-risk cases: unit-test generation, documentation, code explanation, refactoring suggestions, static analysis support and developer knowledge retrieval.

I would measure actual outcomes such as cycle time, defect rate, review effort and developer adoption.

The rule would be simple: AI can accelerate engineering work, but the engineer and the team remain accountable for the code that reaches production.


CATEGORY 10 — RELIABILITY, INTEGRATION AND OBSERVABILITY

47. What happens when NPHIES or another external platform is unavailable?

I design external dependencies assuming they will eventually fail.

For NPHIES or another external platform, I would use timeouts, circuit breakers, controlled retries with backoff, durable queues where appropriate, idempotency and clear transaction state.

The user and operations teams need visibility: is the transaction accepted, pending, failed locally or waiting for an external system?

I would also define reconciliation and replay procedures so recovery does not create duplicates.

This matches how I think about production accountability today: release, monitoring, supportability, continuity and recovery are part of the solution, not operations work that starts after development ends.

48. How do you prevent duplicate claims or financial transactions?

I use idempotency as a core transaction design principle.

Each claim or financial transaction should have a stable business identifier or idempotency key. Repeated requests should return or continue the same logical transaction rather than create another one.

I also use state machines, database constraints where appropriate, deduplication checks and reconciliation against external acknowledgements.

Retries must be designed with duplicate risk in mind.

In financial and healthcare workflows, “at least once” delivery without idempotent processing is dangerous. Reliability is not only making sure a message arrives; it is making sure it does not create the wrong business result twice.

49. How would you architect end-to-end observability for RCM?

I would design observability around the business transaction, not only around servers and APIs.

A claim should have a traceable journey across services and external systems using a correlation or transaction ID.

I want structured logs, distributed traces, technical metrics, dependency health and business metrics such as transaction latency, queue age, rejection reasons, retry volume and failure stage.

My current operating model already includes monitoring and logging, capacity and performance, incident escalation and RCA. For an RCM platform, I would take that one step further into business observability.

The best incident view should answer: which customer, which claims, what impact, where is the failure and what can we safely replay?


CATEGORY 11 — DELIVERY, DECISION-MAKING AND CHANGE MANAGEMENT

50. How do you balance architecture quality with delivery speed?

I do not see architecture quality and delivery speed as opposites.

The goal is to put strong guardrails around the things that are expensive to get wrong—security, data ownership, interfaces, reliability and irreversible decisions—and give teams freedom inside those boundaries.

I use reference patterns, early reviews, automated tests and release gates so quality is built into delivery rather than checked at the end.

In my current environment, production readiness includes QA/UAT, performance and security validation, observability and supportability. That may look slower than “just ship,” but it reduces rework and production failure.

Good architecture should make normal delivery faster.

51. How do you make decisions when you do not have complete information?

I first classify the decision by risk and reversibility.

If it is easy to reverse, I am comfortable making the decision with enough evidence and learning quickly. If it is expensive or dangerous to reverse, I spend more time validating assumptions.

I make the unknowns explicit, get input from the people closest to the problem, and define what evidence would change the decision.

My architecture work often includes current-to-target decisions, build-versus-buy, TCO, PoCs and production transition. Those decisions rarely have perfect information.

The skill is not waiting for certainty. It is making a responsible decision with visible assumptions and a clear way to learn.

52. Tell me about a decision you changed.

I would answer this with a real decision that I can defend in detail rather than invent a story.

A strong example should show that I made an initial decision based on the information available, received new evidence, changed direction without protecting my ego, and improved the outcome.

The structure I would use is: the original decision, why it was reasonable, the new evidence, what I changed, the impact and what I learned.

I have many complex programmes—MedIQ replacement, SAP integration, Dynamics 365 and platform modernization—but I would only use one as this example if I can describe the actual decision change accurately.

The leadership point is that changing a decision when evidence changes is strength, not inconsistency.

53. How do you get senior engineers to adopt an architecture they disagree with?

I start by making sure resistance is being heard, not managed away.

Senior engineers often see risks that leadership may miss. I ask them to challenge the assumptions, architecture boundaries, NFRs and operational impact with evidence.

If their case is stronger, I change the design.

If we keep the original direction, I explain the decision criteria and involve them in making the implementation successful.

I also prefer small validation steps or PoCs for high-risk changes instead of forcing belief through authority.

Adoption is much stronger when people understand the trade-offs and have influenced the design, even if their preferred option was not selected.

54. How do you modernize while the business keeps demanding new features?

I avoid treating modernization as a separate multi-year programme that delivers no business value until the end.

I connect modernization work to real product and operational pain: a feature that is difficult to change, an integration bottleneck, scaling limit, reliability issue or high support cost.

Then I modernize incrementally around those areas while new business features continue.

That is the approach I have used while moving established solutions toward newer web, API, database, container and analytics technologies without disrupting operations.

I also make technical debt visible in portfolio planning so it competes transparently for capacity rather than becoming invisible work engineers do at night.


CATEGORY 12 — ORGANIZATION, GOVERNANCE AND METRICS

55. How do you measure architecture success?

I measure architecture through outcomes, not the number of diagrams or standards.

Useful measures include time to onboard a customer, integration reuse, lead time for change, deployment frequency, change failure rate, incident severity, recovery time, performance, cost to operate and the amount of customer-specific code.

I also look at architecture risk: unsupported technology, single points of failure, key-person dependencies, unclear data ownership and high-coupling areas.

In my current role, architecture is connected to production through service KPIs, release gates, incident and RCA and technical-debt management.

The business should feel that architecture makes growth easier, not that Architecture is producing more governance artifacts.

56. What can you do that our existing architects cannot?

I would not assume that SANTECHTURE’s existing architects are missing a capability before I understand the team.

What I bring is additional scale and breadth: I have moved from hands-on engineering into enterprise architecture and then into executive ownership of a 54-person multidisciplinary technology organization.

I have led national-platform replacement, enterprise integration, data and master-data governance, modernization, production operations, budget and vendor decisions.

So my value is not to be “smarter than the architects.” It is to connect architecture across product, engineering, operations, customers and executive decisions, and to help strong architects operate as a coherent function.

I would first learn where the team is already strong and then add leverage where it is needed.

57. What would you change at SANTECHTURE today?

I would not walk in with a list of changes before understanding the actual system and organization.

Public information can tell me what SANTECHTURE sells, but it cannot tell me where the real internal friction is: architecture coupling, release process, customer customizations, integration ownership, observability, technical debt or team boundaries.

In the first weeks, I would build a current-state view and identify where architecture is creating the highest cost or risk.

Only then would I recommend change.

My experience has taught me that a technically correct target state can still fail if the transition path, ownership and production impact are not understood.


CATEGORY 13 — FIRST 90 DAYS AND CEO ALIGNMENT

58. What would you investigate first if you joined us?

I would investigate six things first.

Four: the rules and regulatory-change lifecycle. Five: architecture debt and the parts of the system that slow releases or create incidents. Six: the organization—decision rights, ownership and how Product, Sales, Architecture and Engineering work together.

I would also review recent major incidents and strategic customer escalations because they often reveal architecture problems faster than diagrams.

I would listen before proposing a redesign.

59. What would your first 90 days look like?

My first 90 days would have three phases.

I would spend time with Product, Sales, Delivery and customer-facing teams, not only Engineering.

From days 31 to 60, I would create an architecture baseline: major capabilities, system boundaries, integration map, NFR risks, ownership and a prioritized list of areas to keep, improve, modernize or replace.

From days 61 to 90, I would want at least one visible improvement delivered—not only a document. It might be an integration standard, better claim-level observability, a modernization pilot or stronger quality gates.

By day 90, leadership should have a clear architecture baseline, priority roadmap and confidence that Architecture is helping delivery move faster and more safely.

CATEGORY 14 — FAILURE, SELF-AWARENESS AND PRESSURE QUESTIONS

61. Tell me about a failure.

I would not invent a failure for the interview.

I would choose a real example where the outcome was below expectation and where I personally owned part of the decision.

My answer structure would be: what I decided, why it looked reasonable at the time, what went wrong, the impact, what I personally owned, what I changed and what I do differently today.

The important part is to avoid a fake failure that is really a strength.

Before the interview, I would select one real case from my history that I can discuss comfortably under follow-up questions. The value of the answer is ownership and learning, not the size of the failure.

62. What is your biggest weakness?

One weakness I actively manage is that, because I grew from hands-on engineering, I can naturally go very deep into technical detail when a problem is important.

That depth helps in architecture reviews and major incidents, but at executive scale it can become a bottleneck if I stay involved longer than necessary.

I manage it by being explicit about decision boundaries, delegating implementation ownership and asking myself whether my involvement is improving the decision or simply making me feel closer to the technical work.

Leading a 54-person organization has made that discipline much stronger.

I still want technical context, but I do not need to own every technical answer.

63. What would your team say about you?

I think they would say I am demanding about ownership and production quality, but fair and technically engaged.

They would probably say I ask many questions when a decision has major risk, and that I expect people to explain the business and technical reasoning rather than simply say, “this is the standard.”

I also think they would say I give strong people room to own their areas and that I am available when there is a serious problem.

Of course, I would not claim every person experiences my leadership in exactly the same way. But clarity, accountability, technical context and support are the characteristics I try to be consistent about.

64. What are your salary expectations?

I am primarily evaluating the scope, team, decision authority and overall opportunity.

Given the seniority of the role, I would expect a competitive Director-level package aligned with SANTECHTURE’s structure and the market, including the full compensation package rather than only base salary.

I am happy to discuss numbers once I understand the complete role, expectations and package.

My goal is to reach a fair level for both sides, not to make compensation the first reason for the move.
