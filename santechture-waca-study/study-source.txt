TRACK ONE
SANTECHTURE — DIRECTOR OF SOLUTIONS ARCHITECTURE


Question 1. Tell me about yourself.

I’m a technology and digital transformation leader with more than 18 years of experience.

I started as a software developer and moved through team leadership, enterprise applications, architecture, and then executive technology leadership.

Today, I lead Information Systems and Digital Transformation at Egypt’s Unified Procurement Authority. I lead a 54-person multidisciplinary technology team, with seven direct reports, and an annual IT budget of around 150 million Egyptian pounds.

A major part of my recent work is MedIQ, a national healthcare procurement and medical-supply platform serving more than 38,000 users, 11,000 healthcare facilities, and 2,000 suppliers.

What I bring is a combination of technical depth, architecture, production accountability, and leadership.

For SANTECHTURE, I’m interested in using that experience in a product-driven healthcare company where architecture directly affects customers, scale, and business results.


Question 2. Why leave an executive role for this one, and will you stay?

I’m not making the decision based on title.

I already have a senior title and a broad scope. What I’m looking for is a different kind of challenge and a role where I can create strong impact.

If I make the move, I would make it seriously and with the intention to stay.

SANTECHTURE brings me closer to product strategy, architecture, customers, engineering, and G C C growth.


Question 3. You do not come from our exact domain. Why should we hire you?

That’s fair. I would not claim experience I don’t have.

What I do bring is strong healthcare technology experience, complex integrations, production operations, security, vendors, data, architecture, and leadership.

I learn the domain by working closely with the people who know it best, then I help turn that knowledge into reliable systems and repeatable practices.

For R C M, I would rely on SANTECHTURE’s domain experts for the deepest rules, and make sure that expertise becomes scalable, testable, and observable in the platform.


Question 4. What would your first 90 days look like?

In the first 30 days, I would learn: the people, systems, customers, current architecture, recent incidents, and major constraints.

By day 60, I want a clear baseline of risks, ownership gaps, and the highest-value improvements.

By day 90, I want at least one visible improvement delivered, and an agreed roadmap with measurable priorities.

At SANTECHTURE, I would spend a lot of time with Product, Engineering, Sales, and Delivery, especially around onboarding, integrations, reliability, and cost to serve.


Question 5. How do you approach disaster recovery, R T O, and R P O?

I start with business impact.

How long can this service be unavailable, and how much data can we afford to lose?

That gives us the R T O and R P O.

Then I design the recovery controls around those targets: redundancy where needed, backups, restore testing, dependency mapping, monitoring, and runbooks.

The important point is that a successful backup job does not prove we can recover the service.

For SANTECHTURE, the targets should reflect claims continuity, payer dependencies, customer S L A commitments, and revenue impact.


Question 6. How do you approach identity and privileged access?

My baseline is simple: multi-factor authentication, least privilege, role-based access, separate admin accounts, regular access reviews, and auditable privileged activity.

I want security controls to be enforceable technically, not only written in policy.

For SANTECHTURE, I would also make data residency, retention, and administrative access part of the product architecture.


Question 7. How do you design integrations?

I start with ownership, data contracts, and failure behavior.

Then I define authentication, validation, versioning, mappings, error handling, monitoring, and reconciliation.

My rule is that the next integration should be easier and safer than the previous one.

In my current environment, I’ve applied this across Dynamics 365, healthcare systems, G S 1, and other national integrations.

At SANTECHTURE, I would turn that into reusable adapters and stable internal models, so hospital, payer, and regulatory variation stays outside the product core.


Question 8. A critical system or external dependency is down. What do you do?

First, I confirm impact and severity, assign one incident owner, and communicate clearly.

Then I restore the safest critical service or workaround first.

For external dependencies, I design for failure with timeouts, controlled retries, queues where appropriate, and clear transaction state.

After recovery, I run root-cause analysis and track the corrective actions.

For NPHIES or payer platforms, I also need to know which claims are pending, failed locally, or waiting on the external service, so we can replay safely.


Question 9. Why SANTECHTURE?

What attracts me is the combination of healthcare, product engineering, and regional scale.

SANTECHTURE is using deep R C M expertise, rules, and artificial intelligence to improve claim quality and reimbursement outcomes across G C C markets.

That creates a very interesting architecture challenge: how do you scale domain intelligence, integrations, and automation without losing reliability or auditability?

That is exactly where my background in healthcare platforms, integration, and production architecture is relevant.


Question 10. What do you understand about SANTECHTURE’s business?

I see SANTECHTURE as a healthcare revenue-cycle technology company.

The business value is reducing administrative effort, improving claim quality, lowering rejection, and making reimbursement faster and more predictable.

Technically, the challenge is combining rules, workflow, data, and artificial intelligence with hospital, payer, and regulatory integrations across different G C C markets.


Question 11. Why should I hire you?

Because I bring a combination that is difficult to find in one profile: engineering roots, enterprise and solution architecture, healthcare technology, complex integration, production accountability, and executive leadership.

I can discuss value and risk with executives, work with customers on discovery and feasibility, and still go deep with engineering on boundaries, data, security, and reliability.

I would not replace your R C M experts. I would help scale their expertise through architecture and engineering.


Question 12. How would you scale the platform across hospitals and G C C markets?

I would keep one strong product core and control variation at the edge.

Market, payer, and hospital differences should be handled through configuration, rules, adapters, and defined extension points, rather than customer-specific forks.

I would also standardize onboarding, testing, and observability.

My target would be simple: hospital number one hundred should be easier to onboard than hospital number ten.


Question 13. How do you approach enterprise pre-sales?

My background is technical solutioning rather than carrying a sales quota.

I start with discovery: what outcome the customer wants, their current workflow, systems, integrations, data, volumes, security, and regulatory constraints.

Then I separate what is configuration, what is integration, what is a true product gap, and what is customer-specific.

I would rather surface a constraint early than win a deal with a promise Engineering cannot support.


Question 14. A strategic customer asks for a customization that damages the product. What do you do?

I separate the business need from the implementation they asked for.

First, I look for configuration, an existing capability, or a reusable extension.

If it is genuinely unique, I evaluate the revenue, strategic value, support cost, security, and long-term product debt.

I want to solve the customer’s problem, but I would not damage the core product for one short-term commitment.


Question 15. How would you structure Solutions Architecture?

I would make Solutions Architecture accountable for end-to-end technical outcomes, not just diagrams.

It should sit close to Product, Engineering, Sales, and Delivery, and own major solution patterns, integrations, non-functional requirements, architecture decisions, and technical pre-sales where appropriate.

Decision rights need to be clear.

I would measure success through things like onboarding speed, reuse, reliability, and cost to serve.


Question 16. How would you build the technical roadmap?

I would start from business strategy: target markets, customer needs, product priorities, and the constraints limiting growth today.

Then I would define the target capabilities we need across software as a service, rules, integrations, data, artificial intelligence, security, and platform engineering.

After that, I would sequence the roadmap by value, risk, and dependency.

Every major architecture item should have a measurable business or engineering outcome.


Question 17. How would you modernize a legacy platform?

I would modernize where the current architecture creates measurable pain: slow releases, tight coupling, scaling problems, reliability issues, or too much customer-specific change.

I prefer incremental modernization, often using a Strangler approach, while protecting production continuity.

I would use microservices only where independent scale, ownership, or deployment justifies the added distributed-system complexity.


Question 18. Product wants it now, Engineering says three months, and Sales promised six weeks. What do you do?

I bring everyone back to the same business outcome.

What must actually be delivered in six weeks?

Why does Engineering need three months?

What is unsafe or non-negotiable?

Then I create options: smaller scope, phased delivery, reuse, or a revised commitment.

The final date must be one the company can own together, not one department’s promise.


Question 19. How would you manage millions of rules and regulatory change?

I would treat rules as governed product assets, not scattered code.

Every rule needs an owner, version, effective date, payer or market scope, test coverage, audit history, and rollback.

High-impact changes should go through automated regression and, when useful, simulation against historical transactions.

We should always be able to answer which rule produced a decision and why.


Question 20. Where should artificial intelligence be used, and where should it not?

I would use artificial intelligence where prediction and pattern recognition create value: denial risk, anomalies, documentation assistance, coding suggestions, and prioritization.

I would keep deterministic controls where correctness and auditability must be guaranteed.

My preference is progressive automation: more automation where confidence is high and risk is low, and stronger human review where the decision has high impact.


Question 21. How would you approach R C M observability?

I want to trace the claim end to end.

A claim should have a correlation identifier across services and external systems.

Then I want technical telemetry plus business signals: latency, queue age, rejection reason, retries, and failure stage.

During an incident, we should be able to answer very quickly: which customer, which claims, where did it fail, and what can we safely replay?


Question 22. How would you approach H L seven and FHIR interoperability?

I would treat interoperability as a product capability, not a collection of customer-specific interfaces.

I want versioned contracts, adapters, validation, terminology mapping, and a stable internal model.

For FHIR, supported resources and profiles should be explicit.

My strongest experience is broader healthcare and enterprise integration, so for deep FHIR specialization, I would work closely with the right domain experts rather than overclaim.


Question 23. How do you handle G C C data residency and compliance?

I treat compliance as an architecture input from day one.

I would map what data we hold, where it can be stored and processed, retention requirements, and cross-border restrictions for each market.

Then I translate that into encryption, access control, audit, data minimization, and monitored administration.

Legal and Security interpret the regulation. Architecture makes it enforceable and testable.

I would use mobile device management to enforce the important baseline: enrollment, encryption, screen lock, compliance rules, controlled applications, and remote wipe where appropriate.

I would rather manage controlled exceptions than weaken the baseline for everyone.


Question 16. How would you handle unstable connectivity or power in a mission?

First, I identify what must continue during the outage.

Then I use the simplest safe continuity option: offline capability where the application supports it, local continuity where appropriate, or a controlled manual fallback.

When connectivity returns, synchronization and reconciliation need to be controlled.

The solution must be secure, but it also has to be supportable in the actual field environment.
