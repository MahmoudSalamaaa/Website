SIAD HOLDING — 35 INTERVIEW PRACTICE QUESTIONS — SIMPLE SPOKEN ENGLISH.
Prepared for Assistant Director of Digital Platforms and Engineering.
These are practice questions, not confirmed questions from the interview panel. The answers distinguish my real experience from plans for SIAD.

Question 1. Tell us about yourself.

I started as a software developer and systems analyst. I later led twenty developers in Oman, delivering government and enterprise platforms. Today I lead a multidisciplinary technology organisation of fifty-four people, with nine direct reports and an annual IT budget of about 150 million Egyptian pounds. I contributed to the original MedIQ platform and later led its re-architecture and replacement. I also led major SAP and Dynamics 365 integrations. I bring hands-on engineering experience and practical leadership to help SIAD deliver reliable digital products.

Question 2. Why this Assistant Director role?

I enjoy turning business needs into reliable digital platforms. This role combines architecture, engineering execution and team leadership. I can bring my experience in complex platforms while learning SIAD's tourism and fleet businesses from their owners. I can connect requirements analysis, architecture, software engineering, operational reliability and executive governance.

Question 3. How will you work with Mr Ibrahim Alharbi?

I would agree clear priorities, decision rights and a simple reporting rhythm. I would own day-to-day engineering delivery, raise risks early and bring options rather than surprises. Strategic decisions stay aligned with his direction.

Question 4. What does being number two in technology mean to you?

I would report directly to Mr Ibrahim and support his priorities. My job would be to turn his strategy into clear engineering plans, lead daily delivery, solve problems early and keep him informed. I would take decisions within my agreed authority and escalate major risks with options.

Question 5. How do you lead 54 people?

I lead through nine direct reports, with clear service owners, measurable goals and regular reviews. Our fifty-four-person organisation includes engineering, infrastructure, security, data, applications and support; they are not all developers. I delegate technical details to specialists, remove blockers and stay accountable for delivery and service quality.

Question 6. How do you handle a weak engineering manager?

I start with evidence and clear expectations. I ask what blocks performance, agree a time-bound improvement plan and offer coaching. I review results fairly. If risk continues, I follow formal HR procedures.

Question 7. How do you manage engineering capacity?

I map demand, critical skills, dependencies and operational support load. I protect capacity for security, incidents and technical debt. I make trade-offs visible to business owners before promising dates.

Question 8. How do you create engineering accountability?

Each service needs an owner, quality criteria and an escalation route. Teams should know who decides architecture, who deploys, and who supports production. I measure outcomes, not just task completion.

Question 9. How do you manage conflict between teams?

I first separate facts from assumptions. I clarify the shared business outcome and decision rights. I bring the relevant owners together, document the decision and check its effect after implementation.

Question 10. Explain an architecture decision you made.

One important decision was how to replace the MedIQ core without stopping critical services. We chose a controlled transition instead of a sudden switch. We prepared data mapping, migration checks, parallel operation, cutover and support. I led the architecture and delivery decisions with our technical specialists. The priority was safe change and correct data, not speed alone.

Question 11. How do you design an enterprise API?

I start with the business process, data owner and users. I define the API contract, authentication, validation, error responses, audit logs and testing. I personally designed and developed healthcare REST APIs using JSON over HTTPS and token-based access. The larger enterprise integrations were delivered with my teams.

Question 12. Tell us about SAP integration.

I led the UPA side of a completed, greenfield, two-way integration with the SAP Strategic Medical Warehouses platform. We agreed requirements, system boundaries, master-data mappings, interface contracts, testing and reconciliation. I coordinated go-live and support. I did not configure SAP itself.

Question 13. Monolith or microservices?

I choose based on team capability, domain boundaries, scaling and operational cost. A modular monolith can be safer for a smaller team. Microservices make sense when independent deployment and clear ownership justify the added complexity.

Question 14. Tell us about MedIQ replacement.

I worked on MedIQ from its early development and later led the team responsible for replacing its core platform. We prepared data migration, checks, testing, training, parallel operation and a controlled cutover. The platform has more than thirty-eight thousand users. Our organisation received a Pharmaconex digital transformation award in 2026 for this work.

Question 15. How do you decide go-live readiness?

I check business acceptance, critical tests, security, data accuracy, monitoring, rollback and support readiness. I present a clear go or no-go recommendation with risks and options. Final approval must follow the company's agreed decision process.

Question 16. What would your first 90 days at SIAD look like?

In the first thirty days, I would meet the teams and business owners, understand each platform, and review risks and delivery promises. In the next thirty days, I would agree owners, key measures and the most urgent fixes. By day ninety, I would aim to show early improvements and agree a realistic roadmap with Mr Ibrahim.

Question 17. How do you handle a delayed programme?

I identify the critical path, root cause and actual business impact. I compare options with the team, replan dependencies and escalate changes early. I never hide a delay behind a green dashboard.

Question 18. How do you improve uptime?

I start with service criticality, monitoring and incident history. Then I review single points of failure, capacity, backup, recovery testing and support ownership. I would never promise an uptime percentage without measured evidence.

Question 19. How would you handle a major incident?

First, I check who is affected and appoint an incident lead. We protect users and data, restore service safely and give regular updates. After recovery, we find the root cause and test the fix. In MedIQ, we also completed a planned disaster recovery test using an alternate environment. That was a test, not an unplanned outage.

Question 20. How do you embed security in delivery?

I include access control, threat review, secrets handling, dependency checks and testing in the delivery process. Security and infrastructure specialists review the technical controls. I remain accountable for business risk and release decisions. In MedIQ, one-time-password multi-factor authentication and a user activity log were rolled out. I oversee security and release controls with accountable specialist teams.

Question 21. What is your approach to disaster recovery?

I start by asking which services are most critical and how much downtime and data loss the business can accept. Then we agree recovery targets and test backups, dependencies and recovery steps with the infrastructure team. We carried out a planned MedIQ recovery test in an alternate environment. I would only quote recovery numbers that have been measured.

Question 22. How do you evaluate a vendor?

I evaluate technical fit, security, integration constraints, total cost, delivery and support. I require early technical validation and measurable acceptance tests. In a previous CRM project, I learned not to rely on supplier commitments before validating the architecture against our data-center and connectivity constraints.

Question 23. Explain your AI experience.

We explored a controlled DeepSeek natural-language-to-SQL capability for MedIQ data. The design focuses on approved read-only SELECT queries, validation, role-based access and logging. I would not describe an explored capability as a fully rolled-out production AI service.

Question 24. How do you govern enterprise data?

I define data owners, business definitions, validation and access rules. Integration is not successful if teams cannot trust the numbers. I use reconciliation and quality checks before executive reporting. I led UPA-side data preparation, mapping and harmonization for the production Central Data Warehouse, and oversaw a separate MedIQ data pipeline and operational reporting patterns.

Question 25. How would you approach an OTA platform?

I would first understand the customer journey, supplier contracts and current systems. I would map search, prices, available inventory, payment and booking confirmation. I would focus on correct booking states, reliable supplier APIs and monitoring. I would validate the design with SIAD's product and operations teams before recommending changes.

Question 26. B2B versus B2C platform architecture?

B2C focuses on customer experience, conversion and payment. B2B often needs account contracts, roles, negotiated pricing, invoicing and partner APIs. I would identify shared services while keeping commercial rules clear.

Question 27. How would you avoid double booking?

I would give each booking request a unique idempotency key and keep clear states: pending, confirmed or failed. If a supplier times out, I would check the real booking status before retrying. I would also reconcile payment and booking records so we avoid both double bookings and double charges.

Question 28. How would you design fleet tracking?

I would separate incoming GPS data, vehicle status, alerts and dashboards. Some devices will lose their connection, so the system must show when a location is old and process late data safely. I would protect location privacy, define retention rules and test the design with fleet operations.

Question 29. How would you build shared digital services?

I would identify reusable identity, notifications, payments, integration and observability capabilities. I would define ownership and service contracts. Shared services should reduce duplication without creating a central bottleneck.

Question 30. How do you communicate with executives?

I use short updates: outcome, current status, decision needed, risks and next milestone. I explain technical problems in business language and keep detailed engineering evidence available.

Question 31. What did you do in Oman?

In Oman I led a twenty-person development team working across more than fifteen government and enterprise platforms. I contributed to the National Educational Portal, including learning workflows, reporting and integrations, and to correspondence solutions. I combined requirements analysis, development oversight, testing, releases and customer support.

Question 32. What did Keyframe teach you?

At Keyframe I built my hands-on skills in requirements analysis, C sharp, dot net, databases, reports, testing and deployment. I worked on a wide range of client projects within a portfolio of more than one hundred and fifty engagements. Yashfeen was a healthcare solution linked to recognition for the company through ITIDA.

Question 33. Tell us about a failure.

One example was a CRM implementation in a controlled government environment. I trusted the vendor's commitment too much and did not ask for enough technical validation early in the project. Later, we found that the proposed architecture did not fit our data-center and connectivity constraints. I took responsibility and led the technical assessment and alternative designs. The lesson was clear: vendor promises cannot replace early technical validation. Now I validate critical architecture assumptions before committing to delivery.

Question 34. How would you work with Human Capital Director Tahani?

I would work with the Human Capital Director on hiring, developing managers, performance and succession planning. I would agree the skills we need, set fair expectations and support managers in building strong teams. People development is part of engineering delivery.

Question 35. Why should we hire you?

I bring more than eighteen years of experience across systems analysis, software engineering, architecture and delivery leadership. Today I lead fifty-four people through nine direct reports. I have helped build and replace major platforms and integrate enterprise systems. I can support Mr Ibrahim by turning SIAD's business priorities into reliable products and clear engineering results.
