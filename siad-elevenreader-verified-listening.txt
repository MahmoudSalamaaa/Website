INTERVIEW LISTENING PRACTICE — FINAL REVIEWED EDITION.
THIRTY CORE QUESTIONS AND TEN ADVANCED FOLLOW-UPS.
These are preparation questions, not confirmed questions from the panel. SIAD platform scenarios are hypothetical; career examples reflect documented experience.

PART ONE. THIRTY CORE QUESTIONS.

Question 1. Tell us about yourself and why you fit this position.

I started in software development and systems analysis. In Oman, I led a team of twenty developers. Today, I lead a multidisciplinary technology organization of fifty-four people through nine direct reports. My work includes the MedIQ platform replacement, enterprise integrations, and data platforms. I understand both engineering details and executive delivery. At SIAD, I would focus on reliable platforms, clear ownership, and business results.

Question 2. What would you need to understand before changing SIAD's architecture?

First, I would map the products, users, suppliers, business processes, and current system boundaries. I would review incidents, costs, delivery delays, security risks, and team skills. I would speak with the business and engineering owners. Only then would I recommend changes. I do not want to redesign a platform before understanding what already works.

Question 3. How would you structure engineering teams across OTA, fleet, and shared services?

I would start with clear product ownership. Each team needs a business goal, technical lead, delivery plan, and production responsibility. Shared capabilities should have their own service owners and clear contracts. I would avoid creating a central team that blocks every product. The final structure depends on team size, dependencies, and SIAD's operating model.

Question 4. What decisions should you own, and what should go to Ibrahim?

I would agree decision rights with Mr Ibrahim early. I can own engineering execution, technical reviews, delivery risks, and team performance within my authority. Major investments, strategy changes, and high business risks should be escalated with clear options. I would never surprise him with a problem that I already knew about.

Question 5. Tell me about a major architecture decision you actually led.

During the MedIQ core replacement, we needed to modernize a critical platform while keeping services running and protecting data. We used data mapping, reconciliation, parallel operation, testing, and cutover planning. I led architecture and delivery decisions with technical specialists. The key trade-off was speed against continuity and data integrity.

Question 6. How would you decide between a modular monolith and microservices?

I would start with business domains and team boundaries. A modular monolith is simpler to deploy, test, and troubleshoot when the product and teams are still closely connected. I would choose microservices only when domains are stable, teams can own services independently, and we need separate scaling or releases. I would also assess observability, automated deployment, data ownership, and the cost of distributed failures. I would document the trade-off before deciding.

Question 7. How would you integrate OTA, fleet, ERP, and partner systems?

I would first identify the system of record for each business entity. Then I would define versioned API contracts, authentication, validation, and error handling. For work that can happen later, I would consider queues and events. Retries must be safe, with idempotency keys and reconciliation for failed transactions. I would monitor end-to-end business outcomes, not only whether each API returns a successful response.

Question 8. A booking payment succeeds, but the supplier confirmation fails. What do you do?

I would treat payment and booking as separate states. The system must not show a confirmed booking without supplier confirmation. I would use a unique request key, check the supplier status after timeouts, and reconcile payment records. If confirmation fails, the system needs a clear refund or recovery process, with customer communication and an audit trail.

Question 9. The booking API is slow during a seasonal peak. How would you investigate?

First, I would measure where the delay happens: our API, database, cache, or supplier. I would check traffic, error rates, slow queries, and dependency timeouts. Then I would protect the booking path, adjust capacity, and apply safe fixes. I would measure the result before making larger architecture changes.

Question 10. How would you design a reliable fleet tracking platform?

I would separate device ingestion, location processing, alerts, and dashboards. Devices can go offline, so every location needs a timestamp and a clear stale-data status. We must handle late or duplicate events safely. I would also define access controls, location privacy, and retention rules with the business.

Question 11. What should be shared across SIAD's digital platforms?

Identity, notifications, integration standards, monitoring, and selected payment capabilities may be shared. But I would confirm the business needs first. Each shared service needs a clear owner, service contract, and support model. Reuse should reduce duplication, not create a single point of failure or a delivery bottleneck.

Question 12. How would you improve engineering delivery predictability?

I would start with a visible backlog, realistic capacity, clear priorities, and small delivery milestones. I would track blocked work, defects, and changes in scope. Managers must raise risks early. I would focus on finished business outcomes, not just the number of tasks closed.

Question 13. Two product owners both say their project is urgent. What do you do?

I would compare business value, deadlines, risk, dependencies, and available capacity. I would show the trade-offs openly and recommend an order of work. If the decision affects strategy or commitments, I would take options to Ibrahim. I would not promise the same people to two projects at the same time.

Question 14. How do you reduce technical debt without stopping delivery?

I would identify debt that causes incidents, security risk, slow releases, or high support cost. Then I would include the highest-risk fixes in the delivery plan. Some debt can be addressed during normal feature work. I would explain the business impact rather than asking for a large technical cleanup without evidence.

Question 15. How would you measure engineering performance?

I would use a small set of measures: delivery predictability, production incidents, recovery time, change failures, and customer impact. I would review them with context, not use them to punish teams. The aim is to find bottlenecks and improve service quality.

Question 16. How do you lead managers rather than manage every engineer yourself?

Today I lead fifty-four people through nine direct reports. I agree goals and responsibilities with each manager, review delivery and service risks, and help remove blockers. I expect managers to own technical decisions within their scope. I stay accountable for the overall result without becoming a bottleneck.

Question 17. An engineering manager repeatedly misses commitments. What do you do?

I would check the facts first. Is the problem skill, capacity, unclear priorities, or poor management? I would agree specific expectations, support, and review dates. If performance does not improve, I would work with Human Capital through a fair formal process. The team and business still need a recovery plan.

Question 18. What would you do if Ibrahim requested an unsafe launch date?

I would explain the specific risks in business terms and show the evidence. Then I would offer options, such as reducing scope, adding a controlled pilot, or moving the date. If a critical security or data-integrity issue remains, I would recommend no go. I would document the decision and follow the agreed governance process.

Question 19. How would you handle a major production incident?

I would confirm customer impact, appoint an incident lead, and focus the team on safe recovery. We would protect data and communicate clearly with business leaders. After recovery, we would investigate root causes and verify corrective actions. I have led planned recovery exercises, which are different from an unplanned outage.

Question 20. What is your approach to resilience and disaster recovery?

I would agree business recovery needs for each critical service. Then I would review backups, dependencies, alternate environments, and test evidence with specialists. We completed a planned MedIQ recovery exercise in an alternate environment. Recovery targets should be based on business requirements and measured results.

Question 21. How do you build security into engineering delivery?

I would make security checks part of daily delivery. The pipeline should run automated tests, dependency and secret checks, and basic code analysis. Higher-risk releases need additional security review and evidence. I would also define access control, audit logging, and incident ownership. In MedIQ, our teams introduced one-time-password multi-factor authentication and user activity logging. Security controls must protect the business without making every small change wait for a committee.

Question 22. Tell us about an enterprise integration you personally led.

I led the UPA side of a two-way integration with the SAP Strategic Medical Warehouses platform. We worked on requirements, data mapping, interface contracts, testing, reconciliation, and go-live coordination. I led the integration work on our side; I did not configure SAP itself. I also directed completed Dynamics 365 integration work.

Question 23. What is your hands-on API design experience?

I personally designed and developed healthcare REST APIs using JSON, HTTPS, and token-based access. I start with the business process and data owner. Then I define the contract, validation, errors, access rules, logs, and tests. For large integrations, I lead specialist teams and remain accountable for the end-to-end result.

Question 24. How would you make executive reports trustworthy?

I would define each metric, its data owner, source, and calculation. I would use validation and reconciliation before publishing numbers. In my current work, I led data preparation and harmonization for a Central Data Warehouse. If two reports disagree, I would investigate the definitions and source records before choosing a number.

Question 25. Where would you use AI in SIAD, and what would you avoid?

I would start with a real business problem and a small measurable pilot, such as support knowledge search or controlled analytics. I would protect sensitive data, check accuracy, and keep human review where needed. We explored a read-only natural-language-to-SQL capability using DeepSeek. I would not present that exploration as a full production AI rollout.

Question 26. When would you build software internally instead of buying it?

I would compare business differentiation, time, total cost, security, integration, support, and vendor dependence. I would build when the capability is central to the business and we can own it well. I would buy when a proven product meets the need at lower risk. The decision must include long-term operating cost, not only purchase price.

Question 27. Tell us about a professional failure and what changed afterward.

In a CRM implementation, I relied too much on the vendor's technical commitment. We later found that its architecture did not fit our controlled data-center and connectivity constraints. I took responsibility and led a technical reassessment and alternative designs. Now I require early technical validation before major commitments.

Question 28. How would you handle disagreement with a senior executive?

I would explain the risk calmly, using facts and practical alternatives. In one case, a senior executive requested sharing company-related data with an external party. I raised a governance concern and asked for written authorization before following the formal direction. I respect leadership decisions while making risk and accountability clear.

Question 29. What would you do in your first ninety days?

In the first month, I would learn the products, team, architecture, commitments, and risks. In the second month, I would agree ownership, priorities, and engineering measures with Ibrahim. By day ninety, I would aim to complete a few high-value improvements and present a realistic roadmap, with success measures agreed in advance.

Question 30. Why should SIAD choose you for this role?

I combine hands-on systems analysis and software engineering with large-team leadership. I have led critical platform modernization, enterprise integrations, and multidisciplinary teams. Today I lead fifty-four people through nine direct reports. I can bring clear ownership, sound technical judgment, and disciplined delivery while learning SIAD's products and customers.

PART TWO. TEN ADVANCED TECHNICAL AND HR FOLLOW-UPS.

Follow-up 1. How would you establish one engineering operating model across different products?

I would set a common engineering baseline across products: code review, automated tests, clear environments, release ownership, monitoring, and incident response. Teams can use different technology stacks, but critical quality and security controls should be consistent. I would start with one product, fix the biggest bottleneck, and compare lead time, change failure rate, and recovery time before rolling the model out.

Follow-up 2. How do you choose between two architecture options under time and budget pressure?

I would compare business value, security, delivery time, total cost, operational risk, and flexibility. I would test the riskiest assumptions with a small proof of concept. Then I would document trade-offs and recommend the option with the best balance of value and risk.

Follow-up 3. Cloud spending rises sharply but performance is unchanged. What would you do?

I would separate increased business usage from waste. I would review idle compute, storage, network traffic, and expensive database work. I would protect service reliability while testing cost improvements. Then I would assign cost owners and set budgets and alerts.

Follow-up 4. How would you modernize a legacy platform without interrupting business?

I would map critical business journeys and data dependencies, then move in controlled stages. During the MedIQ core replacement, our approach included data mapping, reconciliation, parallel operation, and cutover planning. I would apply the same discipline at SIAD after validating its real architecture.

Follow-up 5. How would you introduce DevSecOps without creating bureaucracy?

I would begin with a delivery pipeline that automatically builds, tests, and checks dependencies. I would add secret scanning and security checks early, with stronger manual review for high-risk changes. I would make deployment and rollback repeatable, and ensure production monitoring is ready. I would pilot the process with one team and track release speed and defects before expanding it.

Follow-up 6. How would you divide a limited engineering budget?

I would first protect critical operations, security, and contractual commitments. Then I would compare investments by business value, delivery risk, and total cost. I oversee an annual IT budget of about one hundred and fifty million Egyptian pounds today, but SIAD may have a different budget model.

Follow-up 7. Tahani asks: How would you develop engineering managers and retain key people?

I would first identify the skills and leadership gaps with each manager. I would set clear expectations, give regular feedback, and create practical development plans. I would work with Human Capital on fair performance reviews, succession plans, and retention risks. In my current role, I lead fifty-four people through nine direct reports, so developing managers is essential to scaling my leadership.

Follow-up 8. What would you do if releases frequently caused production defects?

I would review incident patterns, test coverage, deployment steps, and rollback readiness. I would automate checks for critical customer journeys and make changes smaller where possible. I would track change failure rate and recovery time, rather than only adding approval meetings.

Follow-up 9. A critical supplier API becomes unavailable during peak demand. What is your response?

I would protect our services with timeouts, controlled retries, and clear failure states. We should not promise bookings that the supplier has not confirmed. I would keep operations and customer teams informed. After recovery, we would reconcile pending transactions and review supplier performance.

Follow-up 10. Tahani asks: Why are you interested in leaving your current role, and what are your expectations?

I value the scale and public impact of my current work. I am interested in SIAD because the role brings together digital products, engineering leadership, and commercial platforms. I want to contribute to a growing business and take clear responsibility for platform delivery. For compensation, I would first like to understand the full responsibilities and package, then discuss a fair level based on the role and my experience.
