SIAD HOLDING. ASSISTANT DIRECTOR OF DIGITAL PLATFORMS AND ENGINEERING.
EXECUTIVE AND TECHNICAL INTERVIEW PRACTICE. REBUILT EDITION.
These are predicted practice questions, not confirmed questions from the panel.
Answers use documented experience where stated. SIAD architecture answers are proposed approaches, not claims about its current systems.

Question 1. Tell us about yourself and why you fit this position.

I started in software development and systems analysis. In Oman, I led a team of twenty developers. Today, I lead a multidisciplinary technology organization of fifty-four people through nine direct reports. My work includes the MedIQ platform replacement, enterprise integrations, and data platforms. I understand both engineering details and executive delivery. At SIAD, I would focus on reliable platforms, clear ownership, and business results.

Question 2. What would you need to understand before changing SIAD's architecture?

First, I would map the products, users, suppliers, business processes, and current system boundaries. I would review incidents, costs, delivery delays, security risks, and team skills. I would speak with the business and engineering owners. Only then would I recommend changes. I do not want to redesign a platform before understanding what already works.

Question 3. How would you structure engineering teams across OTA, fleet, and shared services?

I would start with clear product ownership. Each team needs a business goal, technical lead, delivery plan, and production responsibility. Shared capabilities should have their own service owners and clear contracts. I would avoid creating a central team that blocks every product. The final structure depends on team size, dependencies, and SIAD's operating model.

Question 4. What decisions should you own, and what should go to Ibrahim?

I would agree decision rights with Mr Ibrahim early. I can own engineering execution, technical reviews, delivery risks, and team performance within my authority. Major investments, strategy changes, and high business risks should be escalated with clear options. I would never surprise him with a problem that I already knew about.

Question 5. How would you assess a fragmented platform architecture?

I would draw the main systems and their data flows. Then I would identify duplicated functions, direct dependencies, weak interfaces, and unclear data ownership. I would rank issues by customer impact and operational risk. My goal would be a practical roadmap, not a large redesign with no business value.

Question 6. Tell me about a major architecture decision you actually led.

During the MedIQ core replacement, the challenge was to modernize a critical platform while keeping services running. We chose a controlled transition with data mapping, reconciliation, parallel operation, testing, and cutover preparation. I led architecture and delivery decisions with technical specialists. We balanced modernization speed against continuity and data integrity.

Question 7. How would you decide between a modular monolith and microservices?

I would look at domain boundaries, team ownership, scaling needs, and operational maturity. A modular monolith can be easier to test and operate. Microservices can help when teams need independent releases and the boundaries are clear. I would not choose microservices only because they sound modern.

Question 8. How would you integrate OTA, fleet, ERP, and partner systems?

I would define clear API contracts, data owners, security rules, and monitoring. I would avoid many hidden point-to-point connections. For asynchronous work, I would consider events and queues, but only where they solve a real problem. I would use versioning, retries, and reconciliation so one partner failure does not damage the whole platform.

Question 9. A booking payment succeeds, but the supplier confirmation fails. What do you do?

I would treat payment and booking as separate states. The system must not show a confirmed booking without supplier confirmation. I would use a unique request key, check the supplier status after timeouts, and reconcile payment records. If confirmation fails, the system needs a clear refund or recovery process, with customer communication and an audit trail.

Question 10. How would you prevent double bookings and double charges?

I would use idempotency keys for repeated requests, clear booking states, and supplier-side confirmation checks. A timeout is not proof that a booking failed. Before retrying, we must check the real state. Payment and booking records should be reconciled, and exceptions should be visible to operations.

Question 11. The booking API is slow during a seasonal peak. How would you investigate?

First, I would measure where the delay happens: our API, database, cache, or supplier. I would check traffic, error rates, slow queries, and dependency timeouts. Then I would protect the booking path, adjust capacity, and apply safe fixes. I would measure the result before making larger architecture changes.

Question 12. How would you design a reliable fleet tracking platform?

I would separate device ingestion, location processing, alerts, and dashboards. Devices can go offline, so every location needs a timestamp and a clear stale-data status. We must handle late or duplicate events safely. I would also define access controls, location privacy, and retention rules with the business.

Question 13. What should be shared across SIAD's digital platforms?

Identity, notifications, integration standards, monitoring, and selected payment capabilities may be shared. But I would confirm the business needs first. Each shared service needs a clear owner, service contract, and support model. Reuse should reduce duplication, not create a single point of failure or a delivery bottleneck.

Question 14. How would you improve engineering delivery predictability?

I would start with a visible backlog, realistic capacity, clear priorities, and small delivery milestones. I would track blocked work, defects, and changes in scope. Managers must raise risks early. I would focus on finished business outcomes, not just the number of tasks closed.

Question 15. Two product owners both say their project is urgent. What do you do?

I would compare business value, deadlines, risk, dependencies, and available capacity. I would show the trade-offs openly and recommend an order of work. If the decision affects strategy or commitments, I would take options to Ibrahim. I would not promise the same people to two projects at the same time.

Question 16. How do you reduce technical debt without stopping delivery?

I would identify debt that causes incidents, security risk, slow releases, or high support cost. Then I would include the highest-risk fixes in the delivery plan. Some debt can be addressed during normal feature work. I would explain the business impact rather than asking for a large technical cleanup without evidence.

Question 17. How would you measure engineering performance?

I would use a small set of measures: delivery predictability, production incidents, recovery time, change failures, and customer impact. I would review them with context, not use them to punish teams. The aim is to find bottlenecks and improve service quality.

Question 18. How do you lead managers rather than manage every engineer yourself?

Today I lead fifty-four people through nine direct reports. I agree goals and responsibilities with each manager, review delivery and service risks, and help remove blockers. I expect managers to own technical decisions within their scope. I stay accountable for the overall result without becoming a bottleneck.

Question 19. An engineering manager repeatedly misses commitments. What do you do?

I would check the facts first. Is the problem skill, capacity, unclear priorities, or poor management? I would agree specific expectations, support, and review dates. If performance does not improve, I would work with Human Capital through a fair formal process. The team and business still need a recovery plan.

Question 20. What would you do if Ibrahim requested an unsafe launch date?

I would explain the specific risks in business terms and show the evidence. Then I would offer options, such as reducing scope, adding a controlled pilot, or moving the date. If a critical security or data-integrity issue remains, I would recommend no go. I would document the decision and follow the agreed governance process.

Question 21. What are your go-live criteria for a business-critical platform?

I need business acceptance, tested critical journeys, security review, data checks, monitoring, rollback planning, and a ready support team. Open risks must be visible, with owners. I would give a clear go or no-go recommendation. Final approval follows the company's agreed authority.

Question 22. How would you handle a major production incident?

I would confirm customer impact, appoint an incident lead, and establish clear communication. The technical team would focus on safe recovery while we protect data and limit further damage. After service returns, we would review the root cause and verify corrective actions. Our MedIQ disaster recovery test was planned; I would not describe it as a real outage.

Question 23. What is your approach to resilience and disaster recovery?

First, I would agree which services are critical and what recovery targets the business needs. Then I would review backups, dependencies, alternate environments, and test results with specialists. We completed a planned MedIQ recovery test in an alternate environment. I would not claim specific recovery times without measurements.

Question 24. How do you build security into engineering delivery?

Security should start in design, not only before release. I would require clear access rules, secure secrets, dependency checks, testing, and audit logs. Specialist teams review the controls. In MedIQ, we rolled out one-time-password multi-factor authentication and user activity logging. I would keep security risk visible in release decisions.

Question 25. Tell us about an enterprise integration you personally led.

I led the UPA side of a two-way integration with the SAP Strategic Medical Warehouses platform. We worked on requirements, data mapping, interface contracts, testing, reconciliation, and go-live coordination. I led the integration work on our side; I did not configure SAP itself. I also directed completed Dynamics 365 integration work.

Question 26. What is your hands-on API design experience?

I personally designed and developed healthcare REST APIs using JSON, HTTPS, and token-based access. I start with the business process and data owner. Then I define the contract, validation, errors, access rules, logs, and tests. For large integrations, I lead specialist teams and remain accountable for the end-to-end result.

Question 27. How would you make executive reports trustworthy?

I would define each metric, its data owner, source, and calculation. I would use validation and reconciliation before publishing numbers. In my current work, I led data preparation and harmonization for a Central Data Warehouse. If two reports disagree, I would investigate the definitions and source records before choosing a number.

Question 28. Where would you use AI in SIAD, and what would you avoid?

I would start with a real business problem and a small measurable pilot, such as support knowledge search or controlled analytics. I would protect sensitive data, check accuracy, and keep human review where needed. We explored a read-only natural-language-to-SQL capability using DeepSeek. I would not present that exploration as a full production AI rollout.

Question 29. When would you build software internally instead of buying it?

I would compare business differentiation, time, total cost, security, integration, support, and vendor dependence. I would build when the capability is central to the business and we can own it well. I would buy when a proven product meets the need at lower risk. The decision must include long-term operating cost, not only purchase price.

Question 30. Tell us about a professional failure and what changed afterward.

In a CRM project, I trusted the vendor's technical commitment too much. We did not validate the proposed architecture early enough. Later, it did not fit our controlled data-center and connectivity constraints. I took responsibility and led a technical reassessment and alternative designs. Since then, I insist on early technical validation before major delivery commitments.

Question 31. How do you manage a vendor whose solution is not technically suitable?

I would compare the proposed solution with agreed requirements and constraints, using technical evidence. I would ask for a tested correction or alternatives and assess the impact on cost and schedule. I learned this in the CRM project: supplier promises cannot replace technical validation. I would document decisions and protect the business from further risk.

Question 32. How would you handle disagreement with a senior executive?

I would explain the risk calmly, using facts and practical alternatives. In one case, a senior executive requested sharing company-related data with an external party. I raised a governance concern and asked for written authorization before following the formal direction. I respect leadership decisions while making risk and accountability clear.

Question 33. What would you do in your first ninety days?

In the first month, I would listen to Ibrahim, product owners, and engineering managers, then map platforms, commitments, incidents, and risks. In the second month, I would agree ownership, a small engineering scorecard, and priority fixes. By day ninety, I would aim to deliver early measurable improvements and a realistic roadmap. I would validate the plan before promising results.

Question 34. How would you work with Tahani on engineering capability?

I would agree the skills and leadership roles we need, then review hiring, development, performance, and succession plans with Human Capital. I would set fair expectations and support managers with coaching. The goal is a team that can deliver reliably and retain critical knowledge.

Question 35. Why should SIAD choose you for this role?

I combine hands-on systems analysis and software engineering with large-team leadership. I have led platform modernization, enterprise integrations, and critical service delivery. Today I manage a multidisciplinary team of fifty-four people through nine direct reports. I would bring clear engineering ownership, practical architecture decisions, and disciplined execution while learning SIAD's business in depth.
